A Utopian City should be inspired by Circuits

2026-09-17 · 3 min read

Establishing the Scene

I was recently in New York. What an amazing city! I was walking on the streets of Manhattan, and I realized how efficient the city is. I was stunned by the architecture, the busy vibe, the amazing pizza and, of course, the beautiful women.

Note that I call the city efficient. It’s a strange way to describe a city. In fact, it almost felt like the city had life. One quick walk from Central Park to the Macy’s store made me realize that cities do have life. It feels as if the people walking on the streets are cells of one huge creature made of concrete flesh, connected through the very ground the people walk on. These tiny cells get absorbed and stored overnight, and they get circulated to a different organ when the sun is up. The system works as if the concrete organs are in agreement. The subways can be treated as veins, vessels or nerves. This is one way of looking at New York: a giant concrete creature that uses human beings as cells, who in turn add life to the city.

Another Viewpoint

There’s another way to look at the city: an aerial view. I mean that quite literally. Looking at New York City from an airplane makes it look like a circuit. I am sure that people who have worked with circuits have thought of this. All the buildings resemble resistors and capacitors, and the low, flat buildings look like IC chips. It got me thinking: what if the city was built by a circuit designer? Circuit designers always think of the best way to arrange components with a basic principle in mind. Charge always takes the path with the least resistance. Let’s apply that analogy to the city. Buildings store charge (we humans). The office buildings get charged throughout the day, making them resemble charge accumulators. By the end of the day, the electrons (humans) flow from higher potential to lower potential (houses). The traffic lights are nothing but gates. They can follow the exact rules of logic gates, allowing traffic through based on the combination of inputs. The roads are a straightforward analogue to the PCB rails. All the green trees along the city streets are micro heat sinks, absorbing the heat.

How about designing a city like a circuit

Pause for a second. Think about it. If we use the same design principles that we use to design circuits, we can measure the peak heat dissipated by each component (buildings) and the max current in a wire (traffic), to make the charge (humans) move through the city (circuit) more efficiently.

For circuits, we give the components, connections and circuit size to circuit design software, and the software comes up with an efficient design. We can, of course, make tweaks as needed. Similarly, there could be software where we specify the max and min building count, the max and min building height, the population the city needs to hold, and the material constraints. The software should technically be able to generate an ideal city, placing buildings, houses, trees, subways and traffic signals in the most efficient arrangement for a given population. It could do this to the point where the design cannot be optimized further. If the population increases, the extension to the city could also be derived from the software itself.

The prosperity of a city is literally the measure of the well-being of its people. The well-being of the people is directly proportional to their productivity, income and health. An ideally designed city should solve all these problems, making it a Utopian city. :)
